Abstract

Sign language, the way people actually film it

Current sign language recognition and translation benchmarks are commonly collected in controlled settings with fixed or studio-style cameras, which limits how well they reflect everyday mobile communication. This gap is especially visible for Arabic Sign Language (ArSL), the primary sign language used across Arab countries.

Isharah-Selfie is a large-scale Saudi Sign Language (SSL) dataset recorded entirely with front-facing smartphone cameras under unconstrained selfie conditions: one-handed signing, close-range viewpoints, partial body visibility, camera motion, and heterogeneous device resolutions. Every clip carries gloss annotations for CSLR and Arabic translations for SLT, and we define signer-independent and unseen-sentences protocols to test generalization. Benchmarks show that current RGB- and pose-based methods struggle in this setting, especially on novel sentence compositions.

Motivation

When one hand is holding the phone

Deaf signers commonly use the front camera to talk to deaf and hearing people and to share on social media. Members of the local Deaf community confirmed that one-handed signing is normal in short, informal smartphone interactions.

Conventional CSLR data

Fixed camera · studio / broadcast
  • Both hands visible and free
  • Wide framing, full signing space
  • Static camera, consistent resolution
  • Controlled background and lighting

Isharah-Selfie

Handheld · front-facing smartphone
  • Non-dominant hand removed (holds the phone)
  • Reduced signing space, close-range perspective distortion
  • Frequent hand–face occlusion, partial body visibility
  • Camera motion, 480p–4K, unconstrained scenes

Gloss + Arabic

Dataset construction

From sentence design to verified clips

Sentence selection

1,493 sentences designed with domain experts across seven everyday domains, refined and cross-validated by certified sign language experts.

Data collection

Signers hold the phone in one hand and record 15–25 sentences per session. Each sentence gets ≥5 recordings; a certified ArSL expert records the reference.

Segmentation

Signers pause 2–3 s with hands out of frame; MediaPipe hand-detection gaps mark boundaries for semi-automatic sentence clipping.

Annotation & QA

Two experts annotate gloss and Arabic text. Clips are checked against the reference; deviations are re-recorded or re-verified.

Signers

8 Deaf, 6 hearing ArSL translators, 2 hard-of-hearing. 11 male, 5 female, aged 21–50. Some wear masks or niqab. All gave informed consent and were compensated.

Recording guidance

Portrait orientation, practical selfie distance, signing hand and upper body kept visible, camera movement minimized. Varied clothing, backgrounds and lighting across sessions.

Quality control

Re-recording when the upper body was insufficiently visible or hands left the frame, plus checks on sign order and semantic content.

Evaluation protocols

Two ways to test generalization

🧑‍🤝‍🧑

Signer-independent

Disjoint signers across splits: 11 train (5,997 videos), 3 dev (1,500), 4 test (1,500). Sentences overlap, so models must learn signer-invariant representations.

🧱

Unseen-sentences

Dev and test sentences never appear in training (8,356 / 330 / 311 videos; 49 and 50 novel sentences). Known glosses must be recombined into new utterances.

Responsible release

The dataset contains identifiable videos in sensitive domains (healthcare, legal, emergency), so it will be released for non-commercial research only under a signed usage agreement. Models should not be used for surveillance, biometric identification, or high-stakes deployment without further validation. IRB approved; all signers consented.

Limitations

Male signers and the 31–40 age group are over-represented; the data is ArSL only; minor annotation inconsistencies may remain; one-handed adaptation can make some signs ambiguous; utterances are short; baselines are single runs.
